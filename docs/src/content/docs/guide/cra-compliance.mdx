This page is for the people who run security at a software manufacturer and have
to live with Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), from
11 September 2026. It explains the reporting duty of Article 14 in plain words, with the
article and the paragraph of the Commission's guidance behind each sentence, and then
how [Incidents](/guide/incidents/) carry it. It is not legal advice: the Commission's
guidance quoted here (Communication C(2026) 5252, 27 July 2026) was approved as a draft
and applies once formally adopted, and your legal counsel decides what applies to you.

## Who and what

- **Who:** the *manufacturer*, that is whoever develops a software product, or has it
  developed, and markets it under its name, paid or free (Art. 3(13)). A product that
  integrates open-source components is the integrator's responsibility (Art. 13(5)).
  Open-source *stewards* get a lighter regime from 11 December 2027 (Art. 24(3)).
- **What:** a *product with digital elements*, which includes its remote data processing
  when the product cannot work without it (Art. 3(1), 3(2)). A pure service (SaaS) is not
  a product; it falls under NIS2 (Directive 2022/2555, Art. 23), which has the same
  24 h / 72 h / one-month clocks.
- **When:** the reporting duty applies from **11 September 2026**, to every product in
  scope, including products placed on the market before the rest of the CRA applies on
  11 December 2027, and after their support period ends (Art. 69(3); guidance §210). The
  other duties, fixing vulnerabilities, security updates, the SBOM, the CE marking, apply
  from 11 December 2027 and only during the support period.

## The two things you must report

Article 14 makes you notify, within the deadlines below, two kinds of event you
*become aware of*:

1. **An actively exploited vulnerability contained in your product** (Art. 14(1)). An
   actively exploited vulnerability is one "for which there is reliable evidence that a
   malicious actor has exploited it in a system without permission of the system owner"
   (Art. 3(42)).
2. **A severe incident having an impact on the security of your product**
   (Art. 14(3)): an incident that affects, or could affect, the product's ability to
   protect sensitive or important data or functions, or that led, or could lead, to
   malicious code running in the product or in a user's systems (Art. 14(5)). A
   compromised build chain, signing key or update server is the textbook case
   (Recital 68).

Everything else is handled, not reported: a vulnerability found by a researcher, by a
bug bounty, by a test lab, or merely listed somewhere with a high score, is not
reportable by itself (Recital 68; FAQ 5.2). You may still notify any vulnerability or
incident **voluntarily** (Art. 15); voluntary reports create no new obligations
(Art. 15(5)).

## Which case are you in

The duty reads differently depending on where the vulnerability is and what you are to
the product. Four situations cover almost everything a scanner surfaces.

### 1. A vulnerability in your own code

Your product *is* the vulnerable component: there is no ladder to climb. The
vulnerability is in your product and exploitable in it by construction, so the only
question left is the third one: is there reliable evidence that someone exploited it, in
any installation of your product, without the owner's permission (Art. 3(42))? If yes,
it is reportable: T0, 24 h, 72 h, users informed, final report after the fix. If no, it is
handled under Annex I, Part II: fix it without undue delay, publish an advisory with the
update, and run your coordinated vulnerability disclosure process. You may report it
voluntarily (Art. 15). Nothing is owed "upstream": you are upstream.

### 2. A vulnerability in a dependency

The common case, and the one the ladder below is for. You integrate a component, a
public advisory names a vulnerability in it, perhaps a known-exploited catalogue lists
it. Reportable by you only when it is in a version you ship, can be exploited in your
product, and has been exploited in your product (guidance §218). Otherwise you fix or
mitigate it (Annex I, Part II) and, where you discovered something the maintainer does
not know, you tell them (Art. 13(6), see [below](#telling-the-components-maintainer)).
Worked example: your product embeds a logging library with a public, actively
exploited vulnerability. It is in your product (step 1, yes). If your configuration never
lets attacker-controlled text reach the vulnerable lookup, it cannot be exploited in your
product (step 2, no): you record a `not_affected` statement with that justification,
upgrade in due course, and report nothing; the maintainer already knows, so you owe
them nothing either. If it can be exploited (step 2, yes) but nobody has exploited it in
your product (step 3, no): fix without delay, tell the clients that run it if you choose,
report nothing. If a customer's logs show the exploit against your product (step 3,
yes): reportable; that is also the moment to check whether your own systems were
reached, which would be a separate severe incident.

### 3. Your product is open source

Being open source changes nothing about what is reportable; it changes **who** owes the
report, through three roles (guidance §69–84):

- **Manufacturer**, when you place the software on the market: you sell it, sell
  support or hosting that requires it, charge for a version of it, or otherwise monetise
  it. All of Article 14 applies to you as to any manufacturer, including for a free
  "community" edition published next to a paid one (§76).
- **Open-source software steward** (Art. 3(14), Art. 24), when a legal person publishes
  the software without placing it on the market, intends it for commercial use by
  others, and provides sustained support: hosting, governance, release management,
  paid developers. Stewards have a lighter regime from 11 December 2027 and report
  under Article 14 "to the extent they are involved" (Art. 24(3)): a steward that only
  manages branding and governance reports nothing (§80); one that runs the project's
  build or signing infrastructure reports severe incidents on that infrastructure (§81);
  one that pays developers who maintain the code reports actively exploited
  vulnerabilities and informs users (§82).
- **Neither**, when an individual, or a legal person without sustained support, publishes
  software for free with no commercial intent: no CRA obligation at all (§74, footnote 14).
  A contributor to someone else's project is not a manufacturer of it (Recital 18).

For the integrators of your open-source product, you are the maintainer of case 2:
they must tell you about vulnerabilities you do not know of, and share their fixes
(Art. 13(6)).

### 4. You run a service, not a product

Software your customers only use over the network, never install, is not a product with
digital elements unless it is the remote processing a product cannot work without
(Art. 3(1), 3(2)). A pure SaaS falls under NIS2 instead (Directive 2022/2555, Art. 23),
with the same 24 h / 72 h / one-month clocks and its own national rules. Hibou's
`nis2-article-23` and `national:<code>` regimes exist for this case. A product that
ships with a companion service is in scope for both.

## Third-party components: the ladder

Most of what a scanner finds is in a library you did not write. The Commission's
guidance (§218) says when such a vulnerability is yours to report: you must notify an
actively exploited vulnerability that originates from a component you integrate, **unless**
the vulnerability "(i) cannot be exploited in [your] product with digital elements (e.g.
because the vulnerable code is not reachable) or (ii) has not been exploited in [your]
product". In those two cases it "does not qualify as an actively exploited vulnerability
contained in [your] product" and is not subject to mandatory reporting. You still have
to fix it (Annex I, Part II) and report it to the component's maintainer (Art. 13(6);
see below).

So a known-exploited catalogue entry on one of your dependencies is a signal, not a
report. Three questions, in order, settle it:

| | Question | If the answer is "yes" | If "no" |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Does a version we shipped integrate the vulnerable version? | next question | not reportable; nothing owed to anyone |
| 2 | Can it be exploited in our product? | next question | not reportable; tell the maintainer unless they know |
| 3 | Has it been exploited in our product? (reliable evidence: a customer's logs, your telemetry, a CSIRT) | **reportable** | not reportable; fix without delay; advise clients with the fix |

"Reportable" always means **to the authorities**: your national CSIRT and ENISA, through
the single reporting platform (Art. 14, 16). Clients and the component's maintainer are
separate audiences with separate rules:

| Step | Authorities (Art. 14) | Your clients | Maintainer (Art. 13(6)) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. not in our product | no | nothing | no |
| 2. not exploitable here | no | nothing | yes, unless they already know (a public CVE means they do) |
| 3. exploitable, not exploited here | no; a voluntary report to the CSIRT is allowed (Art. 15) | fix without delay; an advisory with the security update once Annex I, Part II applies (11 December 2027); before that, telling them is your call | yes, if you found it or you patched it |
| 4. exploited in our product | **yes**: 24 h, 72 h, 14 days | **yes**: impacted users, from the first notice on (Art. 14(8)) | as step 3 |

Hibou shows these as a four-step ladder, because "no" to question 2 and "no" to
question 3 lead to different follow-ups; each step is also the VEX statement it means
for the versions concerned. Write every exit down with its reason: "we decided not to
report, because …" is the record an authority asks for.

## Becoming aware: T0

The clocks count from the moment you *become aware*. The guidance (§213) puts that
moment after a prompt initial assessment: you are aware when you have "a reasonable
degree of certainty" that a vulnerability in your product is being actively exploited, or
that a severe incident has compromised the product's security. A full forensic
investigation is not a prerequisite, and the emphasis is on assessing suspicious events
immediately (§214). Awareness can come from anywhere: a customer, a researcher, a
CSIRT, threat intelligence, your own telemetry or scans (FAQ 5.1). Exploitation you
already knew of before 11 September 2026 is not reported retroactively (§217).

In Hibou, T0 is set by the person who records the decision, defaults to that moment,
can be moved earlier, and is frozen once a filing is recorded.

## The clocks

All three go to the CSIRT of the Member State where you have your main establishment,
and to ENISA at the same time, through ENISA's single reporting platform at
[portal.cra-srp.enisa.europa.eu](https://portal.cra-srp.enisa.europa.eu/) (Art. 14(1),
14(7), Art. 16). The platform is a web portal: a representative you registered signs in
with EU Login and fills a form. It has no API; ENISA says it will extend it over time.

| Step | Vulnerability (Art. 14(2)) | Severe incident (Art. 14(4)) |
|---|---|---|
| Early warning, **within 24 h** of awareness | that an actively exploited vulnerability exists; the Member States where the product is known to be available | that an incident occurred; whether unlawful or malicious acts are suspected; the Member States |
| Notification, **within 72 h** of awareness | the product, the general nature of the exploit and of the vulnerability, measures taken, measures users can take, how sensitive the information is | nature of the incident, initial assessment, measures taken and measures users can take |
| Final report | **14 days after a corrective or mitigating measure is available**: description, severity and impact, the actor where known, the update or other corrective measures | **one month after the notification**: detailed description, root cause, mitigations applied and ongoing |

The CSIRT may ask for intermediate status reports (Art. 14(6)). Early warning does not
mean a fix in 24 hours: the clocks are about telling the authorities; the final report
follows the fix.

## Informing your users

After becoming aware, you inform the **impacted users** and, where appropriate, all users,
of the vulnerability or incident and of the mitigations and corrective measures they can
apply, "where appropriate in a structured, machine-readable format" (Art. 14(8)). This is
not conditioned on a fix being available: the first notice may carry a workaround and a
later one the fix. If you fail to inform users in time, the CSIRT may do it for you.

The duty is risk-based and proportionate (guidance §219–221): it does not mean public
disclosure; you may limit detailed information to the customers concerned, especially
for products in sensitive environments, and disclose more broadly once the vulnerability
is addressed. Independently, once a security update is available, Annex I, Part II,
point (4) requires public information about the fixed vulnerabilities, and point (8)
requires an advisory to accompany the update.

## Telling the component's maintainer

This duty is often misread as "tell every library author about every CVE". It is
narrower. When you **identify** a vulnerability in a component you integrate, you report
it to whoever maintains the component, and you share any fix you developed
(Art. 13(6); guidance §222–228). Four limits:

- Only for the **version you integrate**, through the maintainer's disclosure channel
  when it has one, so an unpatched vulnerability is not disclosed prematurely (§223).
- **Not when the maintainer already knows.** You are expected to check public
  vulnerability databases, the project's advisories and its issue tracker before
  reporting; a vulnerability with a CVE, GHSA or ecosystem identifier is one the
  maintainer knows about (§223). So for the logging library of case 2 above you owe its
  maintainers nothing, and certainly not the news that your product was attacked: that
  goes to your CSIRT and your users, not upstream.
- Only vulnerabilities **in the component itself**, not ones that arise from how you
  integrated it with your own code or with other components (§224).
- Not when the component **has no maintainer** any more, or you no longer rely on them
  for fixes (§225); informing the component's other users through community channels
  is encouraged instead.

Sharing a fix means offering it in a usable form, under a licence the maintainer can
accept (§227); they need not take it, and you need not take theirs (§228). Article 13
applies from 11 December 2027.

## What is not required

- **Not every CVE**, however severe, scored or catalogued: the trigger is an actively
  exploited vulnerability that is in, exploitable in, and exploited in your product.
- **Not monitoring** any particular channel in order to become aware (FAQ 5.1).
- **Not a fix within the clocks**: a workaround and an upgrade path are corrective
  measures; the final report follows whichever is available first.
- **Not a prescribed document format** for the user information: machine-readable "where
  appropriate". The regulation names neither VEX nor CSAF; both are reasonable choices.
- **Not automated filing**: the single reporting platform is where a legal act of the
  manufacturer is filed, by a registered representative. It has no machine interface
  and the filing is yours. Hibou links to the portal next to each filing and pre-fills
  what you paste into it.
